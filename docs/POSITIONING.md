# FieldShift: make the next crop choice count

## The promise we can responsibly make
**See what a crop change could mean for your water budget, soil management and first-year costs before committing a field.**

FieldShift helps a farmer and their local adviser turn a broad idea—“try a different rotation”—into a specific option they can question. Keep current practice as the reference. Examine the annual water requirement, the water still missing, the changes in crop families, and the cost assumptions that could make a switch worthwhile or unaffordable.

## Who benefits first
The initial user is a farmer planning summer/winter crops in the irrigated plains of Punjab, Pakistan, supported where possible by a local agronomist or extension adviser. This regional scope is deliberate: crop timing, markets, irrigation and soil management are local. The 10-hectare field in the demonstration is a constructed example, not an interviewed farmer or a validated customer persona.

The adviser is a supporting user: they can examine which assumptions need correction, check local planting windows and help the farmer judge market and agronomic feasibility. A co-operative or extension organisation is a potential pilot partner; no partnership or demand is claimed.

## The decision that matters
Before buying seed or allocating the field, the farmer needs answers to three questions:
1. **Could I bring the rotation closer to the water I can actually make available?** Compare demand and the remaining annual gap against current practice. Do not interpret a zero annual gap as sufficient water on every growing day.
2. **Would the change make financial sense on my terms?** Use local variable pumping costs, buyer/yield assumptions expressed through crop margin, and switching costs. A negative first-year balance is a reason to question or reject the switch.
3. **What would I be asking of my soil and management?** Look for crop-family changes, legume seasons, residue protection and additional management complexity. These are management proxies, not predicted soil fertility, avoided disease or fertilizer savings.

## The demonstration, in farmer terms
A constructed 10 ha Punjab field currently repeats rice and wheat. With the prototype's stated crop coefficients, that sequence asks for 1,650 mm/year. A millet–chickpea–mung–mustard sequence asks for 650 mm/year: 60.6% lower indicative demand. Across 10 ha, the difference is a demand equivalent of 100,000 m³/year. It is not measured water conserved.

Using 2024 NASA POWER rainfall of 871.84 mm, a 65% effective-rain assumption for loam and 500 mm of available usable irrigation, the annual model gap changes from 583 to 0 mm. A zero annual gap can hide seasonal shortages. Under a separate stress test—30% less rainfall, +2°C and only 200 mm of irrigation—the lower-demand option still has a 53 mm annual gap; the current sequence has 1,053 mm. The user can see that a lower requirement does not settle every constraint.

The soil-support proxy changes from 35 to 65. The soil-protection proxy remains 62 in both rotations. We show that unchanged protection explicitly: this example does not establish that every environmental outcome improves.

## The financial conversation
A separate constructed cost example uses PKR 2 per usable m³, a crop-margin change of −PKR 4,000/ha/year before irrigation costs, and PKR 5,000/ha of first-year switching costs. These are invented demonstration inputs, not local price research.

The annual application screen is capped at the available 500 mm. It assumes 500 mm for the current rotation and 83 mm for the alternative, using the model's rounded effective rainfall. That gives 41,700 m³ less hypothetical application across 10 ha, equivalent to PKR 83,400 of variable pumping-cost reduction at the entered unit cost. Subtract PKR 40,000 of crop-margin loss and PKR 50,000 of switching costs: the first-year screen is **−PKR 6,600**.

The decision benefit is seeing the condition for a better outcome: at those pumping/switching assumptions, the farmer could tolerate at most PKR 3,340/ha of crop-margin loss to break even. The entered PKR 4,000/ha loss is too large. Confirm a better buyer price, reduce switching costs, trial a smaller area, consider another rotation, or retain the current practice. None of those choices should be made from the score alone.

## Why NASA belongs in the story
A farmer can enter water and soil constraints, but those entries do not explain the observed rainfall and temperature context. NASA POWER adds dated historical context from MERRA-2 reanalysis. Regional cells make variation inspectable; point requests ground the comparison at the chosen coordinates. Exact sources allow an adviser to challenge the assumptions. Historical climate context is not a forecast, and a coarse cell does not replace a field measurement.

## Language to use
“Could reduce indicative water demand.” “Under your entered cost assumptions.” “This remains a field-validation question.” “What needs checking before planting?” “Keep the current practice as a credible option.”

Avoid “NASA predicts the best crop,” “guaranteed profit,” “water saved,” “proven resilience,” “reduced pesticide use,” “higher yield,” “carbon removed,” “users love it,” or “farmer-validated.” None is demonstrated. Do not claim a percentage chance of winning.

## A plausible adoption path to test
Begin with assisted use during an existing farmer–adviser planning conversation. The adviser checks the crop portfolio and enters local assumptions with the farmer. The farmer receives a printable option and the unresolved checks. Before expanding, measure whether the result is understandable, actionable and economically relevant. Pricing, willingness to pay, distribution partnerships and business viability remain hypotheses; no sales or customer interviews are claimed.
